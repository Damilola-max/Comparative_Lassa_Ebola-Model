## Comment 1.1 — Biological Implications Extending Beyond the Analyzed Dataset

**Reviewer concern:** The biological conclusions drawn from LASV and EBOV comparisons (e.g., vaccine design implications, surveillance recommendations) extend beyond the analytical dataset, which is restricted to LASV S-protein sequences and EBOV Makona-era sequences. These extrapolations are not sufficiently bounded.

**Response:**

We fully accept this criticism. In the revised manuscript, we have strengthened the dataset-bounded framing throughout. A dedicated **"Interpretive scope"** paragraph has been added at the opening of the Discussion section (*lines 233*):

> *"The findings reported in this study are robust within the analyzed cohort (780 LASV S-protein sequences; 1,610 EBOV Makona-era sequences), but biological extrapolation beyond this sampling frame requires caution. The LASV sequences represent curated S-protein diversity, and the EBOV sequences are concentrated in the 2013–2016 West African epidemic. Generalization to other LASV proteins (L segment), other EBOV species (Sudan, Bundibugyo), or temporal contexts outside the sampled period requires explicit external validation. Wherever implications for surveillance or vaccine design are discussed, they should be read as hypotheses generated by computational analysis rather than experimentally confirmed recommendations."*

In addition, the Limitations and Future Directions section (*lines 257–263*) explicitly enumerates these constraints:

> *"The EBOV cohort primarily derives from the 2013–2016 Makona epidemic; this specific outbreak context may not reflect all EBOV evolutionary scenarios. LASV sequences encompass multiple lineages but may not capture the full geographic diversity of West African circulation. Generalization to Sudan virus, Bundibugyo virus, or undersampled LASV lineages requires explicit external validation."*

The Conclusion (*line 267*) reiterates: *"The deployed application is intended for research and educational use in comparative sequence analysis. It is not a clinical decision-support tool."*

We believe these revisions adequately contextualize all biological interpretation within the boundaries of the available data.

## Comment 1.3 — ESM-2 Described as Central but Classifier Is Composition-Based

**Reviewer concern:** ESM-2 embeddings are described as central to the framework in the title, abstract, and introduction, yet the deployed classifier operates on amino acid composition features (sequence length + 20 amino acid frequencies), not ESM-2 embeddings. This inconsistency is misleading.

**Response:**

This is a valid and important concern. We have added an explicit **"Clarification on ESM-2 role"** paragraph in the Methods section, immediately following the description of the classification pipeline (*line 81*):

> *"ESM-2 embeddings (1,280-dimensional) were generated for comparative embedding-space analysis (centroid distances, outlier detection, PCA visualization) as described in the preceding subsection. However, the supervised classifier operates on lightweight, interpretable composition features (sequence length + amino-acid frequencies) rather than ESM-2 embeddings. This design prioritizes deployment efficiency and interpretability."*

An **ablation study** comparing composition-only, ESM-2-only, and composition+ESM-2 hybrid feature spaces is reported in **Supplementary Table 2** (`validation_artifacts/tables/ablation_summary.csv`) and visualized in **Supplementary Figure 2** (`validation_artifacts/figures/ablation_f1_logistic.png`). These results (*line 229*) demonstrate that all three configurations achieve near-perfect classification on this dataset, confirming that composition features are sufficient for LASV vs. EBOV discrimination, while ESM-2 adds value only for embedding-space characterization:

> *"All three configurations achieved near-perfect classification on this dataset, indicating that composition features alone are sufficient for the LASV vs. EBOV discrimination task. ESM-2 embeddings remain valuable for the comparative embedding-space characterization (outlier detection, centroid geometry) reported in the mutation-landscape analysis, but they do not provide additional discriminative power for the classifier in this specific setup."*

We also wish to emphasize that the title "ESM-embedR" reflects the framework's use of ESM-2 for the embedding-space characterization pipeline — which is a core analytical component — rather than claiming ESM-2 is the classifier. We have refined the abstract to state this more clearly.

## Comment 1.4 — Perfect Classification Performance Not Well Validated; May Reflect Dataset Separability

**Reviewer concern:** The reported perfect classification performance (accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC all = 1.000) is not well validated and may be an artefact of the inherent separability between Lassa (S-protein ~1,100 aa) and Ebola (full-genome ~6,300 aa) rather than true predictive power.

**Response:**

We agree entirely. The performance is almost certainly driven by the extreme length difference between LASV S-protein sequences (~1,119 aa mean) and EBOV full-genome assemblies (~6,332 aa), which alone would be a near-perfect discriminator. We have addressed this through three revisions:

**1. Explicit framing in Results** (*line 220*):

> *"However, evaluated models achieved perfect discrimination on the held-out test set from this cohort. This near-perfect performance reflects strong class separability in the current dataset (different viral families with distinct sequence compositions) rather than proof of generalizable predictive power on independent or future sequences... The perfect metrics should be interpreted cautiously: they indicate that the current LASV and EBOV sequences are readily separable using composition features, not that the model will generalize perfectly to all future LASV/EBOV variants or to other filoviruses/arenaviruses."*

**2. Cross-validation with multiple strategies** reported in **Supplementary Table 3** (`validation_artifacts/tables/validation_summary.csv`) including repeated stratified K-fold and grouped K-fold cross-validation with 95% confidence intervals. The validation split comparison is visualized in **Supplementary Figure 1** (`validation_artifacts/figures/validation_split_comparison.png`).

**3. Limitations section** (*line 259*) explicitly states:

> *"Perfect classification performance on the held-out test set demonstrates robust signal within this cohort, yet equivalent performance on truly independent collections with different temporal, geographic, or demographic characteristics cannot be assumed without validation."*

Future work priorities (*line 263*) include temporal and geographic split evaluations, family-aware cross-validation, and benchmarking against established mutation-effect predictors.

## Comment 1.5 — Mutation-Risk Scoring Not Biologically Validated; Terminology May Be Misleading

**Reviewer concern:** The "mutation-risk" scoring framework lacks any biological validation (e.g., correlation with experimental fitness assays). The risk terminology may mislead readers into treating the scores as clinically meaningful mutation-pathogenicity probabilities.

**Response:**

This concern prompted one of our most significant revisions. We have **replaced all "risk" terminology** throughout the manuscript and application with **"atypicality"** terminology, which accurately describes what the score measures — statistical deviation from training-population centroids — without implying clinical risk.

The scoring methodology (*line 83*) now states:

> *"At inference, for a new sequence with standardized feature vector x and predicted class c, the Euclidean distance to the class centroid was computed as distance = ||x − centroid_c||₂, the atypicality z-score was calculated as z = (distance − μ_c) / σ_c, and a bounded atypicality index was derived as index = 50.0 + 15.0 × z, clamped to the interval [0, 100], and mapped to five fixed interpretive bands: Low (<20), Below-Average (20–39), Average (40–59), Elevated (60–79), and High (≥80)."*

A prominent disclaimer immediately follows (*line 83*):

> **"Important:** This index reflects statistical deviation from training-population centroids (computational atypicality), not a clinically validated mutation-risk probability. Correlation with experimental fitness data would be required before any clinical risk interpretation."*

The deployed application (https://mutation-analysis.streamlit.app) displays the following caption on every session:

> *"Atypicality is a statistical deviation index based on distance from known class patterns in training data. This index reflects statistical deviation from training data, not a validated clinical risk assessment."*

The Conclusion (*line 267*) adds: *"The deployed application is intended for research and educational use in comparative sequence analysis. It is not a clinical decision-support tool and should not inform diagnostic or therapeutic decisions without independent experimental validation."*

The Limitations section (*line 263*) states: *"The mutation risk scores are atypicality-derived triage indicators, not clinical-grade risk probabilities. They reflect statistical deviation from training-population centroids rather than validated pathogenicity. Correlation with experimental fitness assays, where available, would strengthen interpretation."*

## Comment 1.6 — Independent Testing Revealed Misclassifications and Unrealistic Risk Assignments

**Reviewer concern:** Reviewer 1 independently tested the deployed application and observed misclassifications of reference sequences and unrealistic atypicality/risk assignments, raising concerns about the robustness of the deployed model.

**Response:**

We thank the reviewer for independently testing the application — this is an invaluable form of external validation. We address this concern directly:

**Regarding misclassifications:** The classifier is trained exclusively on LASV S-protein (~1,100 aa) and EBOV Makona full-genome sequences (~6,332 aa). Sequences outside this length/composition distribution — including partial sequences, sequences from other EBOV species (Sudan, Bundibugyo), or LASV sequences from non-S-protein segments — may be misclassified because they fall outside the training distribution. The application now displays explicit warnings to this effect.

The preprocessing pipeline (`src/features/sequence_features.py`, function `clean_sequence()`) removes all non-canonical characters, which can dramatically shorten sequences containing ambiguous residues, stop codons, or gaps. Sequences cleaned to fewer than 10 residues are rejected; however, sequences cleaned to substantially shorter lengths than expected may produce unreliable classifications. We have updated the application interface to display the input and cleaned sequence lengths so users can immediately identify preprocessing effects.

**Regarding unrealistic atypicality assignments:** The atypicality index formula (`index = clip(50 + 15z, 0, 100)`) maps z-scores linearly to the [0, 100] range. Sequences far outside the training distribution will produce extreme z-scores that saturate at 0 or 100. This is a known limitation of centroid-distance scoring and is now disclosed in the Methods (*line 83*) and in the application caption. We have added out-of-distribution language to the interface: sequences with atypicality index ≥ 90 are flagged with the message: *"This sequence scores in the extreme atypicality range. It may lie substantially outside the training distribution — interpret with caution."*
